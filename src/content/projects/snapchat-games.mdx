<Section size="sub" stage="Overview" title="Snapchat Games at a glance" hideFromNav>
Snap's bet, going into 2025: games are one of the strongest ways to
spark conversation between friends, and bi-directional communication is
what keeps people coming back to Snapchat. I led design for the work
that took Games from a side feature into a scaled product area with its
own dedicated squad, presenting the strategy weekly to Evan and the
exec team.
</Section>

<Section stage="Background" eyebrow="Background" title="Where Games started" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
"Games" on Snapchat was a category of Lenses. They lived inside the
AR Bar on the camera, behind a Games tab in the carousel, sharing
real estate with face filters and effects. The catalog was
hyper-casual: short, single-screen, pick-up-and-play titles like Rock
Blast, Box Boss, and Pool King, built mostly by a small group of
partner developers shipping roughly 20 Lenses a month.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Playing with friends meant Snapping a result" hideFromNav>
There was no live multiplayer and no real turn-based system. If you
wanted to play with a friend, you played a game lens, then sent the
result as a Snap. Your friend opened the Snap, saw a static image of
your score, then had to open the same lens themselves and play. It
was technically turn-based, but it read as "watching a friend's
result" rather than playing together. The whole loop ran on the
Snapping framework, which meant you could also discard and retake
your turn before sending. You could cheat.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="A category stuck in hyper-casual" hideFromNav>
The catalog had a structural problem alongside the UX one. The mobile
games market is split into three slices, and Snap was sitting in the
worst one for retention and revenue.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Where we were" title="Hyper-casual" tone="outline">
    High virality, low retention (one and done), market saturation,
    and only 0.4% of total US mobile gaming revenue. Easy to make,
    hard to monetize.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="The market sweet spot" title="Hybrid-casual" tone="outline">
    Hyper-casual mechanics plus the meta layers of mid-core:
    progression, persistence, narrative. Growing 30%+ year-on-year.
    Where the industry was moving.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Out of reach" title="Mid-core / core" tone="outline">
    Best retention, strong IAP, but expensive to make and dominated
    by AAA titles. Not where a social app starts.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="The signal that said it was worth fighting for" hideFromNav>
Even inside that buried, hyper-casual setup, the engagement data
pointed at real demand. The top retentive game lenses were hitting
around 30% D1 retention without any daily mechanism, and roughly 7%
of Game Lens WAU were playing 5 or more days a week, skewing toward
users with a weaker social graph. There was a real audience here.
The product just wasn't built to serve them, and there was no path
to monetize them.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Pressure-testing the hypotheses with UXR" hideFromNav>
Around the time of my first strategy deck, I partnered with our UXR
team on a foundational gaming study: 1:1 interviews with 11
Snapchatters ages 13-24, half regular Lens-game players, half not,
all of them also playing on at least one competitor platform
(iMessage, Messenger, Roblox, Instagram, TikTok). The study set out
to validate or kill the bets I was making on the direction.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 1" title="The mental-model split was real" tone="outline">
    "Users familiar with Lens games did not strongly associate filter
    games with Snapchat games, because it wasn't clear they could play
    games with friends in chat like on other platforms."
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 2" title="Users expected games in chat" tone="outline">
    "Users had difficulty finding games due to buried entry points...
    Users expected to start games within the chat." Most participants
    would initiate a game by asking a friend in chat first.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 3" title="Live multiplayer was the top ask" tone="outline">
    The most-appealing proposed game types: multiplayer live,
    multiplayer goal-oriented, turn-based, daily challenges,
    simulation, and party games. Synchronous, social formats over
    mindless or solo intellectual ones.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
The research gave the strategy work qualitative grounding to sit
alongside the engagement data. It's not "I had a hunch and execs
trusted it," it's "I built the framing from product instinct and
data, then pressure-tested it with 11 users before scaling the
bets." Specific findings show up next to the decisions they
validated, later in the case study.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Two jobs to be done" hideFromNav>
I framed the work for the team around two jobs games could do for
Snap, and used that framing in exec reviews to scope the year.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 1" title="Connections and Conversations" tone="outline">
    Get friends to play together inside Snapchat. Games as a reason to
    open a chat, then to keep that chat going.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 2" title="Growth and Resurrection" tone="outline">
    Get more Snapchatters to open the app each day, especially users
    with a weaker social graph who aren't pulled in by chat alone.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="Going Big with Games" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
With the engagement data, the research findings, and the two jobs
framed, I needed exec investment to take Games from a side feature
borrowing capacity from Lenses into a dedicated product area with
its own headcount and roadmap. I wrote the long-term strategy deck
for the Games initiative. The goal: align execs around a multi-year
direction, not just the next quarter's roadmap. The deck got buy-in
and excitement from Evan and the rest of the exec team, and Games
was spun out as its own dedicated squad off the back of it.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The category bet" hideFromNav>
The strategic insight: Snap was sitting in hyper-casual (high
virality, low retention, hard to monetize). The industry's actual
sweet spot is hybrid-casual: the pick-up-and-play mechanics of
hyper-casual plus the meta layers of mid-core (progression, upgrades,
narrative). Merge Mansion is the proof point that anchored the pitch:
60M downloads, $600M lifetime revenue. Hybrid-casual was the part of
the market growing at over 30% year-on-year, and Snap's social graph
was a meaningful unfair advantage in that lane.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="Two guiding principles" hideFromNav>
I anchored the strategy on two principles, one for each audience the
work has to serve.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="For consumers" title="A social-first gaming destination" tone="outline">
    "How can Snapchat become THE place people go to for social mobile
    games?" Snap's massive user base and friend graph is the unfair
    advantage over standalone game platforms.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="For developers" title="Handle everything but the game" tone="outline">
    "Our developers should only have to worry about making a good
    game. We should handle the rest." Distribution, monetization, and
    social plumbing become Snap's job.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The four pillars" hideFromNav>
From the principles, four pillars to focus the next year of work
against. The deck assigned a clear design direction to each.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout number="1" title="Monetize existing games">
    Grow Lens+ subscriptions, layer in video and rewarded ads on
    hyper-casual games using formats we already had, instead of
    building net-new ad surfaces.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="2" title="Enable hybrid-casual mechanics">
    Multiplayer as the foundation. Add progression, persistence, and
    a cross-game identity so a session is worth coming back to.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="3" title="Incentivize the developer ecosystem">
    Expand the Partner Developer Program with upfront funding for
    first-time devs and a discovery boost gated on game retention,
    playtime, and social mechanics.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="4" title="Diversify monetization">
    Beyond ads: in-app purchases for progression boosters, plus
    Bitmoji cosmetics that persist across every game on Snapchat.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="An entry-point system, not a single surface" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
With the long-term direction agreed, the next question was where
games actually live in the app. The unlock was treating Games as a
system of entry points matched to the two jobs, rather than fighting
for one home. Each surface earns its place by serving a different
player intent.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Play with friends" title="Chat Drawer, Friends Feed, FAB, Calling">
    Surfaces inside the messaging stack carry live multiplayer,
    turn-based, and leaderboard games. These are where social games
    belong, because the social context is already there.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Open the app today" title="Discover, Spotlight, Topic Chats">
    Content surfaces and Topic Chats carry solo and passive-browse
    games, daily challenges, and chat-relevant games for players whose
    friends aren't online.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 1" title="A stationary entry on Discover" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Discover gave us the traffic, but we had to pick a treatment that
didn't break the feed. I worked through three options with the team:
a pinned pill (confusing UX), a pinned tile (too disruptive to the
page), and a pinned carousel component. We aligned on the carousel:
it lives inside the existing visual rhythm of the page, gives solo
players a predictable place to land, and quietly becomes a natural
home for playable game ads down the line.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 2" title="Connected Lenses, in chat" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Live multiplayer was the most-requested game type in our foundational
research and the most natural fit for how Snapchatters already use
chat. I designed around three ways to play on the same framework:
solo with visual indicators of friends' past plays, multiplayer with
friends (sessions tied to a chat or call so only those people can see
and join), and online matchmaking against strangers as a fallback.
Sessions support spectators, replays of the last game, and ghosts of
friends if no one is live, so the experience degrades gracefully.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Users valued "live" features that allow them to join friends' games
immediately without waiting. Most prefer playing with friends, but
some would play live with strangers if no one was around, similar to
Roblox.
</Quote>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 3" title="Pulling turn-based off the Snapping framework" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Turn-based games were a chat thread in disguise, but they were riding
on the Snapping framework, which let players discard and retake their
turn. In other words, you could cheat. The format was also confusing
to users in our foundational research: participants couldn't tell
that sending a Snap of a game was supposed to initiate a turn-based
session with a friend.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Some users did not think games could be tied to Snapping, as it's
more of a watching experience than playing with friends.
</Quote>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
I pushed to move turn-based onto the live multiplayer format
instead: turns auto-send when you play them, the opponent can watch
a replay, and the game flips into a live session the moment both
players are present. The chat envelope became the surface, showing
whose turn it is, updating in real time, with a Remind CTA to nudge
the other player without leaving chat. A turn becomes a message, not
a Snap.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 4" title="A daily mechanism for solo players" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
With retentive game lenses already at around 30% D1, a daily
mechanism was the obvious next lever for Job 2. The design proposal: a
Daily Challenges row pinned to the front of the Games carousel, a
per-game streak, an auto-Snap to Preview the day's result, and
notifications that fire only on social signal (friends playing the
puzzle), not on the streak itself. The team pushed back on a few of
my early proposals, including a per-game permission widget, and we
landed on a single opt-in modal the first time you enter a game.
Those calls came out of design review and made the final flow
noticeably cleaner.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 5" title="Defending the camera for creation" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The Lens carousel kept attracting long-play content: longer-form
games, playable ads, choose-your-own-adventure stories. I drove a
content strategy realignment with the cross-functional teams: the
camera should be for creation, and content earns its place in the
carousel only if it drives a Snap create, send, post, or save.
Long-play content lives on Discover and Spotlight instead. As a
follow-on, I scoped a Games Spotlight tab with
continue-where-you-left-off and a Browse Games entry point from any
fullscreen game unlock, so a player who arrives via a friend's score
can pivot into the broader catalog.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 6" title="Fullscreen games and Presence" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The gameplay had to earn the next session, not just the first tap.
Fullscreen Games on iOS replaced the camera-shrunk layout with an
immersive, full-viewport game lens. I designed a unified
tap-to-record behavior so the same control either starts the game
silently or starts it with hands-free recording, then auto-progresses
to Preview when the round ends, so players can clip and send their
best moments without breaking out of the game. Games Presence UI
surfaces the bitmoji and name of friends currently in a multiplayer
game, in friends feed and chat, so an empty lobby is the exception,
not the default.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Users thought it was appealing to see their friends' Bitmojis
associated with games in order to know their friends are interested
in that game and potentially open to playing it together. Make the
experience feel more like playing together — video recording
gameplay, showing a live score count in chat.
</Quote>

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" title="What's next" hideFromNav>
The work going forward is about depth: more partner-built games on
the live multiplayer framework, sharper discovery in the storefront
and a top-level Games Spotlight tab, an in-app points and IAP system
to diversify monetization beyond ads, and tightening the loop between
playing a game and posting about it. The through-line stays the same:
games are at their best when they give friends a reason to talk.
</Section>
