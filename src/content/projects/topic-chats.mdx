<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
Topic Chats existed when I took it over: a public-conversation MVP for
Snapchatters to talk about anything trending. The surface worked. The
product did not. Most chats opened with a wave of "hi" posts and went
quiet.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
The "hi" posts looked like a content problem until I pulled the
timestamps. They clustered inside ten-minute windows: people dropped
in, scanned for a live conversation, and left when they did not find
one. The product did not have a discovery problem. It had a
concurrency problem. We needed enough people in the same chat at the
same time for it to feel alive.
</Section>

<Section stage="Inflection" eyebrow="Inflection" title="Betting the product on live moments" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Inflection" hideFromNav>
I cut the data by category to find where the product was already
working. Generic interest chats were the worst offenders on the
hi-and-leave pattern: people did not know what to talk about. Sports
were the opposite. On-topic, time-bound, and naturally concurrent.
That was the wedge.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Inflection" hideFromNav>
I reframed the roadmap around a specific wedge: live events first,
sports as the proving ground. That narrowed what the product had to
be good at: real-time relevance, repeat participation, and the loops
that move users from one session to the next.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>

    Sports as the proving ground. Inherently time-bound, naturally
    on-topic, and the format most likely to produce concurrent

  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Loops" title="Utility, not chatter" tone="outline">

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Design" title="Designing for a live signal" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Once live events were the wedge, the most leveraged work was making
the chat feel unmistakably live. Two interventions carried most of
that weight: a real-time scoreboard inside the chat itself, and live
state visible from outside the chat so users could find their way back.
</Section>

<TwoUp ratio="2:1" align="center" title="A scoreboard, not a decoration">
  <Fragment slot="left">
    What made the scoreboard work was that it grounded the chat in
    something live that was actually happening right now. Score, clock,
    and key moments update in real time and anchor the conversation as
    the game plays out, so the chat is reacting to a real event instead
    of trying to invent one. It was also shareable: every scoreboard
    moment is a natural beat worth sending to a friend, which turned
    the chat into a source of distribution instead of a dead end.
  </Fragment>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" title="Live state, visible from outside the chat" hideFromNav>
The scoreboard solved the inside. The harder problem was that
Snapchat had no shared language for "live." A chat could be on fire
and nothing in the rest of the app would tell you. I treated live as
a property the app needed to carry, not a state buried inside one
surface, and designed a single live signal the Friends feed, Discover,
Search, the Map, and iOS Live Activity could all speak. The goal was
one glance, anywhere in the app, to know something is happening now.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Design" title="Notifications, reframed as utility" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The hardest problem in public chat is response timing. If users do
not know a chat has come alive after they leave, the conversation
dies. The inherited model pinged you about a person (someone
replied). I redesigned it to ping you about a moment (something is
happening worth being part of), and split that into four categories
ordered by how relevant each one is to any given user, most to least.
</Section>

<Section stage="Supporting" eyebrow="Supporting moves" title="Smaller bets that held the system together" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Supporting" hideFromNav>
Three more pieces rounded out the system. None carried the strategy
on their own, but each one was necessary for the live-events bet to
compound.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Supporting" title="Send limits, to break the hi-and-leave pattern" hideFromNav>
The original chat had no rate limit, which is part of why first-time
joiners could post a "hi" and vanish before anyone replied. A flat
cap would have punished the regulars who were actually carrying the
conversation, and a join-gate would have killed the top of the funnel.
I designed a rate limit that treats first-time posters differently
from regulars: you earn the ability to keep posting by engaging with
the chat, not by waiting your turn. The gate is on participation,
not posting.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Supporting" title="Sharing and invite loops" hideFromNav>
The old share dead-ended at a screenshot. I redesigned around three
jobs: send a live moment that's worth opening, land the receiver
inside the live context, and keep the link alive when it leaves the
app. The point was to turn conversation into distribution instead of
dead-ending chats inside Snapchat.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Supporting" title="Mark as Off Topic" hideFromNav>
The default ask was a reporting queue with human review. I pushed
against it. At live-event concurrency, a queue would either drown
the moderators or slow the signal to useless. Letting the room
decide, with a threshold and a temporary ban for repeat offenders,
made moderation a property of the chat instead of a backlog behind
it. Any user can hide a spammy or irrelevant message for themselves,
and once enough users do, the message is hidden for everyone.
</Section>

A new engagement surface for Snapchat, driven by live-event

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" hideFromNav>
Topic Chats became a stronger product once it stopped trying to be
everything at once. Narrowing to live sports and events gave the team
a wedge they could actually optimize against, and the loops that
followed, the scoreboard, the live-state surfaces, the smarter
notifications, the moderation, and the share mechanics, were the
system that made that wedge compound into a real engagement surface
for Snapchat.
</Section>

<Section stage="Results" eyebrow="What's next" title="The next chapter of the strategy" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" hideFromNav>
Sharing was the weakest loop in the system: roughly 30 shares per
week on the old surface. The rework shipped late, so the next chapter
is proving the growth loop, alongside three in-progress bets that
extend the live-events thesis into discovery, content supply, and
interaction.
</Section>
