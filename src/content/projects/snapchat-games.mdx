<Section size="sub" stage="Overview" title="Snapchat Games at a glance" hideFromNav>
I lead design for Snapchat Games. Over the last year I designed the
Games storefront on Discover, the chat-envelope model for turn-based
games, the fullscreen + tap-to-record system that pulled gameplay out
of the camera-filter frame, and the long-term Games strategy that got
approved by Evan and resulted in Games being spun out as its own
dedicated squad. The product area now sits at 30M+ DAU.
</Section>

<Section stage="Background" eyebrow="Background" title="Where Games started" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
Coming in, Snapchatters mentally split "filter games" on the camera
from "Snapchat games" played with friends in chat. Discovery was thin,
the storefront was buried, and the games surface didn't reflect how
social the format actually is. There was real signal in the data
though: retentive game lenses already hit around 30% D1 retention
without a daily mechanism, and roughly 7% of Game Lens WAU were playing
5 or more days a week, skewing toward users with a weaker social
graph. Games could pull two distinct levers at once.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" eyebrow="The framing" title="Two jobs games can do for Snap" hideFromNav>
Everything in this case study traces back to one of two jobs to be
done. I used this framing in exec reviews to scope the year, and I'll
mark which job each decision serves as the case study goes on.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 1" title="Connections and Conversations" tone="outline">
    Get friends to play together inside Snapchat. Games as a reason to
    open a chat, then to keep that chat going.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 2" title="Growth and Resurrection" tone="outline">
    Get more Snapchatters to open the app each day, especially users
    with a weaker social graph who aren't pulled in by chat alone.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="Going Big with Games: a deck I had to write twice" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" eyebrow="THE CHALLENGE" hideFromNav>
As the early bets started showing engagement, I needed exec investment
to take Games from a borrowed-capacity feature to a dedicated product
area. I wrote a strategy deck. The first version did not get approved.
The second did, and Games was carved out as its own dedicated squad
off the back of it. The gap between those two versions is the part of
this work I'm proudest of, because the design vision underneath did
not really change. What changed was how I led with it.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" eyebrow="V1" title="A designer's vision" hideFromNav>
The first pitch opened with two principles, one for consumers and one
for developers, and then dove straight into the UX: three ways to
play, Connected Lenses for multiplayer, ghosts of friends, spectator
mode, voice chat over a session. Monetization was a big-swing concept
called Snapchat Arcade, with pay-to-play tickets and prizes.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="For consumers" title="A social-first gaming destination" tone="outline">
    "How can Snapchat become THE place people go to for social mobile
    games?" Snap's massive user base and friend graph as the unfair
    advantage over standalone game platforms.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="For developers" title="Handle everything but the game" tone="outline">
    "Our developers should only have to worry about making a good
    game. We should handle the rest." Distribution, monetization, and
    social plumbing become Snap's job.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" eyebrow="INSIGHT" title="Lead with the bet, not the build" hideFromNav>
The execs didn't disagree with the UX. They didn't have a place to
put it. Without a category bet up front, "make Snapchat the place
people go to for social mobile games" sounds like a feature ask, not
an investment thesis. The Arcade concept also landed too far out: a
monetization rewrite when we hadn't proved the retention curve. I'd
led with what I knew best (the design system, the user flows) and
buried the part that actually decides whether a deck gets approved.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" eyebrow="V2" title="Same vision, different framing" hideFromNav>
I rewrote the pitch around the category opportunity first. Snap was
sitting in hyper-casual: high virality, low retention, hard to
monetize. The industry's actual sweet spot is hybrid-casual, the
pick-up-and-play mechanics of hyper-casual plus the meta layers of
mid-core. Merge Mansion was the proof point I anchored on: 60M
downloads, $600M lifetime revenue. Hybrid-casual was the part of
the market growing at over 30% year-on-year, and Snap's social graph
was a meaningful unfair advantage in that lane.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
With category established, the four pillars and the monetization plan
became consequences of the bet, not big swings on their own. The same
UX vision from V1, the multiplayer framework, the in-chat games,
became "Pillar 2: enable hybrid-casual mechanics." The Arcade idea
got demoted into a phased rollout: existing ad formats first,
progression IAP and Bitmoji cosmetics later. Nothing about the design
work changed. The story around it did.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout number="1" title="Monetize existing games">
    Grow Lens+ subscriptions, layer in video and rewarded ads on
    hyper-casual games using formats we already had.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="2" title="Enable hybrid-casual mechanics">
    Multiplayer as the foundation. Add progression, persistence, and
    a cross-game identity so a session is worth coming back to.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="3" title="Incentivize the developer ecosystem">
    Expand the Partner Developer Program with upfront funding for
    first-time devs and a discovery boost gated on retention and
    social mechanics.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="4" title="Diversify monetization">
    Phased: existing ad formats now, IAP for progression boosters and
    Bitmoji cosmetics later.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" eyebrow="IMPACT" hideFromNav>
V2 got approved. Games was carved out as its own dedicated squad,
headcount and roadmap ownership followed, and I now run a bi-weekly
review of this area with Evan and the rest of the exec team. The
shorter version of the lesson: when I'm asking for investment, lead

the UX vision for after the bet is real.

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="An entry-point system, not a single surface" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
Coming out of the approved strategy, the question became where games
actually live in the app. The unlock was treating Games as a system
of entry points matched to the two jobs, rather than fighting for
one home. Each surface earns its place by serving a different player
intent.
</Section>

  <Callout eyebrow="Open the app today · Job 2" title="Discover, Spotlight, Topic Chats">
    Content surfaces and Topic Chats carry solo and passive-browse

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 1 · Serves both jobs" title="Games should feel like games, not filters" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE CHALLENGE" hideFromNav>
A game lens that shares the screen with the camera UI never reads as
a game. It reads as a filter that happens to have buttons. The
default Snapchat capture behavior makes this worse: tap takes a
photo, long-press records video, and the user's intent gets misread
the moment they start playing. To make Games a destination instead
of an accessory, the gameplay layer itself had to break out of the
camera frame.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE SOLUTION" title="A fullscreen lens, a unified capture button" hideFromNav>
Game lenses open fullscreen on start. The capture control is
unified: a single tap starts the game silently, a long-press starts
the game and starts hands-free recording, and the round
auto-progresses to Preview when it ends so the clip is ready to send
without leaving the game flow. Two interactions, two intents, no
extra chrome.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="IMPACT" hideFromNav>
After pulling capture into the moment of play, Game Video Posts on
iOS were up 120%. The shipped behavior also became the basis for
how every new game lens onboards into the system, so the gain
compounds with every new title in the catalog.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 2 · Serves Job 1" title="Social games belong where friends already are" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE CHALLENGE" hideFromNav>
The Friends Feed is where Snapchatters live. If social games belong
anywhere, they belong there. The harder question was how to surface
games without turning the Feed into a games tab, and without giving
every play format (turn-based, live multiplayer, solo) its own
entry point.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE SOLUTION" title="One shortcut, one drawer, every format" hideFromNav>
I designed a Games shortcut in the Friends Feed that opens a drawer
of game activity: turn-based games waiting on you, live multiplayer
sessions friends just started, and a Browse Games entry into the
broader catalog. The drawer became the spine that holds every
social play format, instead of giving each format its own surface.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" title="Inside the rhythm of the page, not on top of it" hideFromNav>
For the games entry on Discover, I worked through three placements:
a pinned pill (looked like a filter chip), a pinned tile (too
disruptive to the page rhythm), and a pinned carousel component.
The carousel shipped because it sits inside the visual rhythm
Discover already has, gives solo players a predictable place to
land, and quietly becomes a natural home for playable game ads
later. We aligned on it by walking through how the page reads on a
normal scroll, three times in a row, with the PM and the
tile-system EM.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 3 · Serves both jobs" title="Live, turn-based, and daily are three modes of one conversation" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE CHALLENGE" hideFromNav>
Live multiplayer, turn-based, and daily solo play were being
designed as three separate products on three separate frameworks.
That created three problems at once: turn-based games could be
cheated, live sessions couldn't gracefully degrade to solo play,
and the daily mechanism had no path back into the social fabric of
chat. The fix wasn't three better features. It was one framework.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="THE SOLUTION" title="Connected Lenses with three modes" hideFromNav>
I designed live multiplayer, turn-based, and daily solo play as
three modes on top of one Connected Lens framework. They share
state, presence, and the path back into chat. A turn-based game
becomes a live session the moment both players are present. A solo
daily run can surface to a friend as a score to beat. A live
multiplayer match can spectate out into the broader friends feed.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="Mode A · Live multiplayer" title="A session is a chat, not a destination" hideFromNav>
Sessions are tied to a chat or call, so only those people can see

the host can start a new round at any time. If nobody is live and
the game supports it, you play against ghosts of friends' past runs
instead of an empty lobby.

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="Mode B · Turn-based" title="A turn is a message, not a Snap" hideFromNav>
Turn-based games were riding on the Snapping framework, which let
players discard and retake their turn before sending. In other
words, you could cheat. I pushed to move turn-based onto the live
multiplayer format instead: turns auto-send when you play them,
the opponent can watch a replay, and the game flips into a live
session the moment both players are present. The chat envelope
became the surface, showing whose turn it is, updating in real
time, with a Remind CTA to nudge the other player without leaving
chat.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" eyebrow="Mode C · Daily" title="Solo play should still feel social" hideFromNav>
With retentive game lenses already at around 30% D1, a daily
mechanism was the obvious next lever for Job 2. The design: a Daily
Challenges row pinned to the front of the Games carousel, a
per-game streak, an auto-Snap to Preview the day's result so the
share moment is one tap, and notifications that fire only on
social signal (friends playing the puzzle), not on the streak
itself. Solo play becomes a daily reason to open the app, but the
social loop is what makes it stick.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" title="Clarity beats cleverness, especially on permissions" hideFromNav>
My first proposal for permissions was a per-game activity-sharing
widget that fades in when you enter a game and fades out after two
seconds. Design review pushed back: a widget that disappears feels
clever, not clear, and players couldn't tell what they had agreed
to. We landed on a single opt-in modal the first time you enter any
game, with a clear explanation of what's collected and how it's
shared. The lesson stuck: on permissions, clarity beats cleverness
even when the cleverness is technically less intrusive.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" title="And one organizational outcome" hideFromNav>
The approved Going Big with Games strategy got Games carved out as
its own dedicated squad. Headcount, roadmap ownership, and a
bi-weekly review cadence with execs all followed. The team I
worked across (PM, engineering, data science, UXR, partnerships)
now has formal ownership of the area instead of borrowing capacity
from Lenses.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" title="What's next, and what I'm least sure about" hideFromNav>
The bet I'm most uncertain about going into the next phase is the
in-app points and IAP system. The category analysis says
hybrid-casual needs progression and persistence to retain, and IAP
needs progression boosters and cosmetics to monetize. But Snap's
audience hasn't paid for in-app items at scale before, and the
Bitmoji-cosmetics path puts our identity layer in front of a
purchase decision for the first time. I'd rather over-test that one
before scaling it.
</Section>
