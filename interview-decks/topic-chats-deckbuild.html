Slide design system — template-aligned, with deliberate breaks
Baseline (the template)
Full aurora background. Eyebrow top-left in mono caps, page number bottom-right (mono). Title in Instrument Serif — italic for hero/single-word/big-metric moments, roman for declarative two-line headlines. Body in Geist sans. Phones orthogonal in device frames; annotations via thin gray leader lines.
Typography hierarchy
Italic serif — covers, single-word titles, big metric numerals (47%, $100M), one-liners where rhythm matters. Roman serif — declarative 2-line headlines. Bold sans — alternate top headline when the slide already has a strong italic element below (metric grids, diagrams). Mono caps — eyebrows, page numbers, photo annotations, axis labels, attributions.
Visual treatment
Phones orthogonal by default (template style), device-framed. Bare screenshots in soft surface cards work for data viz. Tilts only for break slides or scrapbook moments (cover photo cluster) — 2–4° max. Leader lines hairline gray, sans labels. Soft pastel fills (peach/lavender/blue) for diagram shapes. Mono LABEL inside shapes.
Color
Aurora bg on every content slide (powder blue → lavender → peach). One filled pill or one accent fill per slide max. The kill slide is the only deliberate dark-surface — and it should look like a kill, not template chrome.
Archetypes (mapped to your template page numbers)
Cover · template p1 — italic serif title centered/left, eyebrow above, full aurora.
Declarative headline · p2 — roman serif 2-line, eyebrow above.
Big metric 3-up / 4-up · p10–12 — italic serif numerals; bold sans alt headline.
Single phone + leader callouts · p18 — device-framed, orthogonal, hairline leaders.
Two / three phones with captions · p19, p20 — captions above each phone, orthogonal.
Diagram + text · p15–17 — funnel/donut/venn, soft pastel fills, mono LABELs.
Timeline · p13–14 — italic years above a hairline rule with markers.
Quote · p23 — left vertical hairline rule, hanging quote, mono attribution.
People grid · p24–25 — circular avatars (used for appendix research panel).
⌖ Deliberate template breaks (3 max per deck)
Slide 3 · The reframe
Pure typography canvas. Strike "Not a content quality problem." in muted neutral. Drop "A topic problem. / A concurrency problem." in roman serif, anchored low. No aurora, no decoration. Staff signal: she leads with the conclusion, then proves it.
Slide 10 · Five surfaces
Break the orthogonal grid. Five phones staggered on a soft diagonal with varied Y. Scoreboard phone slightly larger, anchors lower-left. Hairline mono leader lines name each surface. Talk track names the principle: same signal, different contract per surface. Staff signal: systems thinking in one frame.
Slide 11 · Earn the entry point
Full-bleed scoreboard (the bet, not the buried entry — visual must enact the claim), dimmed to 60%. Roman serif headline overlaid, italic on "Earn the entry point." Staff signal: the judgment derives from the diagnosis, "you don't scale a broken loop."
Three mistakes to avoid when breaking template
1. Breaking more than 3 slides — if everything's hero, nothing is. The template is the baseline; breaks are punctuation.   2. Breaking for decoration, not argument — every break is tied to a beat (reframe / system / claim-not-apology). Never tilt for cool.   3. Half-committing — if a numeral bleeds, bleed it 15–20%, not 3%. Timid breaks read as mistakes.

Talk track (HM-expanded)
"Same signal, different contract per surface. The signal — that something is happening now — is one thing. The contract is what each surface owes the user: Friends feed is social, so the live pill sits next to people you already know. Discover is editorial, so live chats surface as curated picks. Search is intent-based, so live chats lift above evergreen ones. The Map is ambient and geographic, so live events appear as pins. iOS Live Activity is system-level, so the signal travels off-app to the lock screen. Five surfaces, five contracts, one signal. The hard part wasn't designing the badge — it was getting the badge to read correctly in every context without losing meaning. One glance, anywhere in the app, to know something is happening now. That's the design principle the whole system is built on."
Patreon tie-back
"For Patreon, this maps to whether 'creator is live' or 'new drop' is a first-class signal that respects each surface's contract. Home feed contract is social proof; creator page contract is intent; push is interruption. Same signal, three different jobs — and the trust problem is the same: never let the signal mean different things in different places, or users learn to ignore it."
Pinterest tie-back
"The underlying mechanic: a single discovery signal carries a different contract per surface, and the design job is to honor each contract without fracturing the signal. For Pinterest specifically — a 'fresh pin' badge means something different on the home feed (algorithmic surfacing) than on a board (creator intent) than on search (query relevance). Treating those as three different signals is what produces inconsistent recommendation experiences across the app. Treating them as one signal with three surface-specific renderings is the lever. That's the work I'd want to do."

Reddit tie-back
"For Reddit this is retention infrastructure. Home feed, search results, push, lock screen — every place a cold-landing user touches the product has to signal 'this thread is alive' in the appropriate idiom. Stress-test / objection cheat sheet
"A decision where data and judgment disagreed?"
Volume data pointed at generic interest chats — they had the most members on paper. Judgment (and the dual reframe from the timestamps and chat-content read) said sports had the two properties that actually mattered: topic specificity (people know what to say because the game is the anchor) and natural concurrency (everyone shows up at the same time because the game has a schedule). I picked the wedge with both signals over the wedge with the bigger headcount. Validated by the +32K DAU from notifications and the 52K peak concurrent in a single March Madness chat.
"Why is the entry point buried? Why not fight for something prominent?"
"User research surfaced people had trouble finding the entry point, and I took that signal seriously. I explored more prominent placements early on, but I made the judgment call quickly — and it derived directly from the reframe. The reframe says the problems are topic specificity and concurrency. A more prominent entry point would grow top of funnel, but it would also compound both: more users finding broad-topic empty rooms means more one-and-done participation, more disappointed cold landings, more trust burned. You don't scale a broken loop, you fix it. So I sequenced. Solve the underlying problems first via Problems 1 and 2 — the scoreboard solves topic + live-anchor inside the chat, the live-state signal solves concurrency-discovery across surfaces. Then Problem 3 is re-entry: mechanisms that pull users back at moments when both problems are already solved. Notifications turned out to be the biggest lever at +32K DAU. Once the loop was demonstrably working, the entry-point conversation became a much stronger one — with real numbers behind it."
Tone: the judgment derives from the reframe, not from politics. "Scaling before solving compounds the problems" is the staff move — name the systems-level reason, not just "we didn't have the numbers yet." That second framing reads as compliance with a constraint; the first reads as product judgment.
"What would you do with another quarter?"
"Now that the product has earned the right, the entry point itself is the next unlock. Here's the sketch." (Have a 1-slide sketch ready in appendix.) Don't lead with this, close with it. Lead = apology. Close = roadmap.
"What was the strongest argument against the sports wedge?"
Probably: sports narrows the TAM and locks us into seasonal traffic. Counter-argument was right on its face. What would have changed my mind: if the category analysis hadn't shown sports winning on both axes (topic specificity and concurrency) as cleanly as it did, or if the operating cost of live-event integrations had been prohibitive. Neither was true. The wedge let us learn the loop where both signals were already solved, then generalize the playbook — scoreboard anchor + live-state signal + notification taxonomy — to other live moments after.
"You picked sports over generic interest chats even though the data showed generic interest had the most members. Walk me through the conversation where someone senior pushed back on that, and what specifically changed their mind."
⚠ Prep a real answer. HM-flagged as the hardest panel question. The current "data vs judgment" answer (above) explains your reasoning but not the room. Staff candidates can re-enact the room. Three things to prep:

1. Name the person — actual PM, eng lead, or exec who pushed back, not "the team."
2. Name their specific objection — not "they preferred volume" but the precise argument they made (e.g., "we'll never hit DAU targets on seasonal traffic," or "sports narrows the brand").
3. Name the artifact that turned the argument — the chart, the demo, the pilot data, the qualitative quote. The moment the argument turned, not the conclusion you both eventually reached.

If the call was actually uncontested, that's a tell — but you can still recover by naming who you proactively went to before they could push back, and what you brought them. Without a named partner, this answer reads as either lucky or unowned.
Tone: re-enact the room, don't summarize the reasoning. "I went to [name] with [artifact] because I knew [their objection] would be the hardest one to defend. What turned it was…"
