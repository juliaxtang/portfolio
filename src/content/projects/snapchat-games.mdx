<Section size="sub" stage="Overview" title="Snapchat Games at a glance" hideFromNav>
Snap's bet, going into 2025: games are one of the strongest ways to
spark conversation between friends, and bi-directional communication is
what keeps people coming back to Snapchat. I led design for the work
that took Games from a side feature into a scaled product area with its
own dedicated squad, presenting the strategy weekly to Evan and the
exec team.
</Section>

<Section stage="Background" eyebrow="Background" title="Where Games started" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
Coming in, Snapchatters mentally split "filter games" on the camera
from "Snapchat games" played with friends in chat. Discovery was thin,
the storefront was buried, and the games surface didn't reflect how
social the format actually is. There was real signal in the data
though: retentive game lenses already hit around 30% D1 retention
without a daily mechanism, and roughly 7% of Game Lens WAU were playing
5 or more days a week, skewing toward users with a weaker social
graph. Games could pull two distinct levers at once.
</Section>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="Going Big with Games" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
As the early bets started showing real engagement, I wrote the
long-term strategy deck for the Games initiative. The goal: align
execs around a multi-year direction for the area, not just the next
quarter's roadmap. The deck got buy-in and excitement from Evan and
the rest of the exec team, and Games was spun out as its own
dedicated squad off the back of it.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The category bet" hideFromNav>
The strategic insight: Snap was sitting in hyper-casual (high
virality, low retention, hard to monetize). The industry's actual
sweet spot is hybrid-casual: the pick-up-and-play mechanics of
hyper-casual plus the meta layers of mid-core (progression, upgrades,
narrative). Merge Mansion is the proof point that anchored the pitch:
60M downloads, $600M lifetime revenue. Hybrid-casual was the part of
the market growing at over 30% year-on-year, and Snap's social graph
was a meaningful unfair advantage in that lane.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="Two guiding principles" hideFromNav>
I anchored the strategy on two principles, one for each audience the

</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="For consumers" title="A social-first gaming destination" tone="outline">
    "How can Snapchat become THE place people go to for social mobile
    games?" Snap's massive user base and friend graph is the unfair
    advantage over standalone game platforms.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="For developers" title="Handle everything but the game" tone="outline">
    "Our developers should only have to worry about making a good
    game. We should handle the rest." Distribution, monetization, and
    social plumbing become Snap's job.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The four pillars" hideFromNav>
From the principles, four pillars to focus the next year of work
against. The deck assigned a clear design direction to each.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout number="1" title="Monetize existing games">
    Grow Lens+ subscriptions, layer in video and rewarded ads on
    hyper-casual games using formats we already had, instead of
    building net-new ad surfaces.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="2" title="Enable hybrid-casual mechanics">
    Multiplayer as the foundation. Add progression, persistence, and
    a cross-game identity so a session is worth coming back to.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="3" title="Incentivize the developer ecosystem">
    Expand the Partner Developer Program with upfront funding for
    first-time devs and a discovery boost gated on game retention,
    playtime, and social mechanics.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="4" title="Diversify monetization">
    Beyond ads: in-app purchases for progression boosters, plus
    Bitmoji cosmetics that persist across every game on Snapchat.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="An entry-point system, not a single surface" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
With the long-term direction agreed, the next question was where
games actually live in the app. The unlock was treating Games as a
system of entry points matched to the two jobs, rather than fighting
for one home. Each surface earns its place by serving a different
player intent.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Play with friends" title="Chat Drawer, Friends Feed, FAB, Calling">
    Surfaces inside the messaging stack carry live multiplayer,
    turn-based, and leaderboard games. These are where social games
    belong, because the social context is already there.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Open the app today" title="Discover, Spotlight, Topic Chats">
    Content surfaces and Topic Chats carry solo and passive-browse
    games, daily challenges, and chat-relevant games for players whose
    friends aren't online.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 1" title="A stationary entry on Discover" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Discover gave us the traffic, but we had to pick a treatment that
didn't break the feed. I worked through three options with the team:
a pinned pill (confusing UX), a pinned tile (too disruptive to the
page), and a pinned carousel component. We aligned on the carousel:
it lives inside the existing visual rhythm of the page, gives solo
players a predictable place to land, and quietly becomes a natural
home for playable game ads down the line.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 2" title="Connected Lenses, in chat" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Live multiplayer was the most-requested game type in research and the
most natural fit for how Snapchatters already use chat. I designed
around three ways to play on the same framework: solo with visual
indicators of friends' past plays, multiplayer with friends (sessions
tied to a chat or call so only those people can see and join), and
online matchmaking against strangers as a fallback. Sessions support
spectators, replays of the last game, and ghosts of friends if no one
is live, so the experience degrades gracefully.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 3" title="Pulling turn-based off the Snapping framework" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Turn-based games were a chat thread in disguise, but they were riding
on the Snapping framework, which let players discard and retake their
turn. In other words, you could cheat. I pushed to move turn-based
onto the live multiplayer format instead: turns auto-send when you
play them, the opponent can watch a replay, and the game flips into a
live session the moment both players are present. The chat envelope
became the surface, showing whose turn it is, updating in real time,
with a Remind CTA to nudge the other player without leaving chat.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 4" title="A daily mechanism for solo players" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
With retentive game lenses already at around 30% D1, a daily
mechanism was the obvious next lever for Job 2. The design proposal: a
Daily Challenges row pinned to the front of the Games carousel, a
per-game streak, an auto-Snap to Preview the day's result, and
notifications that fire only on social signal (friends playing the
puzzle), not on the streak itself. The team pushed back on a few of
my early proposals, including a per-game permission widget, and we
landed on a single opt-in modal the first time you enter a game.
Those calls came out of design review and made the final flow
noticeably cleaner.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 5" title="Defending the camera for creation" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The Lens carousel kept attracting long-play content: longer-form
games, playable ads, choose-your-own-adventure stories. I drove a
content strategy realignment with the cross-functional teams: the
camera should be for creation, and content earns its place in the
carousel only if it drives a Snap create, send, post, or save.
Long-play content lives on Discover and Spotlight instead. As a
follow-on, I scoped a Games Spotlight tab with
continue-where-you-left-off and a Browse Games entry point from any
fullscreen game unlock, so a player who arrives via a friend's score
can pivot into the broader catalog.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Decision 6" title="Fullscreen games and Presence" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The gameplay had to earn the next session, not just the first tap.
Fullscreen Games on iOS replaced the camera-shrunk layout with an
immersive, full-viewport game lens. I designed a unified
tap-to-record behavior so the same control either starts the game
silently or starts it with hands-free recording, then auto-progresses
to Preview when the round ends, so players can clip and send their
best moments without breaking out of the game. Games Presence UI
surfaces the bitmoji and name of friends currently in a multiplayer
game, in friends feed and chat, so an empty lobby is the exception,
not the default.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" title="And one organizational outcome" hideFromNav>
The Going Big with Games deck got the long-term direction approved

<Section size="sub" stage="Results" title="What's next" hideFromNav>
The work going forward is about depth: more partner-built games on
the live multiplayer framework, sharper discovery in the storefront
and a top-level Games Spotlight tab, an in-app points and IAP system
to diversify monetization beyond ads, and tightening the loop between
playing a game and posting about it. The through-line stays the same:
games are at their best when they give friends a reason to talk.
</Section>
