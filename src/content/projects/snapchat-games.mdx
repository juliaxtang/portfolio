<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Games were just Lenses in that carousel" hideFromNav>
Games were a subset of lenses, mixed into the same carousel as face
filters and world effects. There was no dedicated category for them,
no search that reliably surfaced them, and most people did not know
the names of specific games anyway. You found one by stumbling across
it while browsing. The experiences were hyper-casual: short,
single-screen, pick-up-and-play, and almost entirely solo.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="A barebones turn-based system existed, but almost no one could figure it out" hideFromNav>
There was technically a way to play with friends: open a game lens,
play your turn, Snap the result, send it to someone. Your friend
opened the Snap, saw your static score, then found and opened the
same lens to take their turn.

The whole flow ran on Snapchat's record-and-send framework with no
dedicated invitation UI and no in-product explanation of the loop.
Users who stumbled on a game in the lens carousel saw something that
looked like a game, but had no way to know multiplayer was possible,
let alone how to start it.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" hideFromNav>
Even without dedicated entry points, and despite UX that made the
experience nearly impossible to navigate, users were still actively
seeking games out. This was a signal from an area we had not
invested in at all.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Pressure-testing the hypotheses with UXR" hideFromNav>
I partnered with our UXR team on a foundational gaming study: 1:1
interviews with 11 Snapchatters ages 13-24, half regular Lens-game
players, half not, all of them also playing on at least one
competitor platform (iMessage, Messenger, Roblox, Instagram, TikTok).
The study set out to validate or kill the bets I was making on the
direction.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 1" title="The mental-model split was real" tone="outline">
    "Users familiar with Lens games did not strongly associate filter
    games with Snapchat games, because it wasn't clear they could play
    games with friends in chat like on other platforms."
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 2" title="Users expected games in chat" tone="outline">
    "Users had difficulty finding games due to buried entry points...
    Users expected to start games within the chat." Most participants
    would initiate a game by asking a friend in chat first.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 3" title="People wanted to play with friends" tone="outline">
    The most-appealing formats were social ones: live multiplayer,
    goal-oriented multiplayer, turn-based, party games. Solo or
    mindless formats ranked lowest. The through-line was playing
    with someone, not playing alone.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Early bets" title="Three projects that earned the bigger ask" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
With the data, the research, and the JBTDs lined up, I picked three
projects to ship first. Not because they were the full answer, but
because they were the highest-leverage, relatively lower-effort
bets I could make to test the core question: with a few meaningful
investments, would games actually become a viable engagement driver
on Snapchat?
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 1" title="Games in the chat drawer" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
**Problem:** There was no dedicated entry point to games, and especially
no way to surface games you could play with friends. You had to
stumble across them in the lens carousel. Research confirmed users
expected games to live inside chat, not in a separate destination.

I designed a Games entry in the chat drawer: tap an icon while
you're in any 1:1 or group chat and a drawer opens with games you
can play with those specific people. Turn-based games waiting on
you sit at the top, then suggested games for that chat, then a
Browse Games entry into the broader catalog.

The existence of the drawer made games something you do
mid-conversation, not a separate destination you have to leave
chat for.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Users expected to find games on Snapchat within the chat input bar...
Most users mentioned they would initiate a game with a friend by
asking them first via chat if they are free to play.
</Quote>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
A single experiment on the Games entry point in chat moved Direct
Snap Send Human by +0.136%. That's Snap's top-line communication
metric, and a lift on it from a placement experiment is a
load-bearing data point: it proved Job 1 wasn't a hunch.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 2" title="Fullscreen game UX" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
**Problem:** Games were built on the Lens framework, which meant they
ran on top of the camera UI. For simple tap-to-play games that wasn't
a problem, but for anything with moderate gameplay mechanics, the
camera controls got in the way. The UI said "AR filter" when the
experience was trying to say "game."

I redesigned the game lens experience to open fullscreen on start
and unified the capture control: a single tap starts the game
silently, a long-press starts the game and starts hands-free
recording, and the round auto-progresses to Preview when it ends
so the clip is ready to send without leaving the game flow.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
After pulling capture into the moment of play, Game Video Posts on
iOS were up 120%. The shipped behavior also became the basis for how
every new game lens onboards into the system, so the gain compounds
with every new title in the catalog.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 3" title="Turn-based games, made less broken" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
**Problem:** The turn-based flow was built on Snapchat's record-and-send
framework with no dedicated UI, no invitation mechanic, and no
in-product explanation of how it worked. Most users who stumbled on
a game had no idea multiplayer was even possible. A full refactor
off the Snapping framework would have been the right long-term move,
but it depended on a multiplayer system we hadn't built yet. So I
scoped this as targeted fixes inside the existing framework.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
**Auto-capture at the end of your turn.** The biggest reason turn-based
games never got off the ground was that nothing told you to send your
turn to a friend. I added auto-capture: when your turn ends, we
capture it automatically and prompt you to send it, making the send
step explicit instead of invisible.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
**Snap envelope updates.** In a busy chat, turn-based game snaps blended
in with regular snaps and disappeared. I redesigned the chat envelope
so a game turn reads as a game at a glance: the lens artwork in a card
that calls out "your turn" or "their turn" clearly. Clearer callouts in
the Friends feed also made it easier to track game progress without
losing it in the scroll.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The point wasn't to fix turn-based once and for all. It was to
prove the format had legs if we made it less broken, and to buy
time for the bigger framework rewrite that would land after
strategy. Engagement on turn-based games picked up across the
board after these shipped, which became one of the data points
that justified the bigger investment.
</Section>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="The exec ask: how do we go big with games?" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
The outstanding engagement signals we saw from just three projects
were enough. Evan, the CEO, walked over and asked: **"how can we go
big with games?"** The strategy deck below is my answer to that
question, and the dedicated Games squad is what came out of it.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The category bet" hideFromNav>
The strategic insight that anchored the deck: Snap was sitting in
hyper-casual. The industry's actual sweet spot is hybrid-casual: the
pick-up-and-play mechanics of hyper-casual plus the meta layers of
mid-core (progression, upgrades, narrative).

Merge Mansion is the proof point I anchored the pitch on: 60M
downloads, $600M lifetime revenue. Hybrid-casual was the part of
the market growing at over 30% year-on-year, and Snap's social
graph was a meaningful unfair advantage in that lane.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Where we were" title="Hyper-casual" tone="outline">
    High virality, low retention (one and done), market saturation,
    and only 0.4% of total US mobile gaming revenue. Easy to make,
    hard to monetize.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="The market sweet spot" title="Hybrid-casual" tone="outline">
    Hyper-casual mechanics plus the meta layers of mid-core:
    progression, persistence, narrative. Growing 30%+ year-on-year.
    Where the industry was moving.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Out of reach" title="Mid-core / core" tone="outline">
    Best retention, strong IAP, but expensive to make and dominated
    by AAA titles. Not where a social app starts.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="Two guiding principles" hideFromNav>
I anchored the strategy on two principles, one for each audience the
work has to serve.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="For consumers" title="A social-first gaming destination" tone="outline">
    "How can Snapchat become THE place people go to for social mobile
    games?" Snap's massive user base and friend graph is the unfair
    advantage over standalone game platforms.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="For developers" title="Handle everything but the game" tone="outline">
    "Our developers should only have to worry about making a good
    game. We should handle the rest." Distribution, monetization, and
    social plumbing become Snap's job.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The four pillars" hideFromNav>
From the principles, four pillars to focus the next year of work
against. The deck assigned a clear design direction to each.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout number="1" title="Monetize existing games">
    Grow Lens+ subscriptions, layer in video and rewarded ads on
    hyper-casual games using formats we already had, instead of
    building net-new ad surfaces.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="2" title="Enable hybrid-casual mechanics">
    Multiplayer as the foundation. Add progression, persistence, and
    a cross-game identity so a session is worth coming back to.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="3" title="Incentivize the developer ecosystem">
    Expand the Partner Developer Program with upfront funding for
    first-time devs and a discovery boost gated on game retention,
    playtime, and social mechanics.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="4" title="Diversify monetization">
    Beyond ads: in-app purchases for progression boosters, plus
    Bitmoji cosmetics that persist across every game on Snapchat.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 1" title="Live multiplayer, on Connected Lenses" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The biggest gap the early bets exposed was synchronous play. The
research said live multiplayer was the most-wanted game type, and
there was nothing like it on Snap. With the squad formed and Pillar
2 of the strategy green-lit, I designed Connected Lenses for
Multiplayer as a single framework with three modes: solo play with
visual indicators of friends' past runs, multiplayer with friends
(sessions tied to a chat or call), and online matchmaking against
strangers as a fallback.

Sessions support spectators, replays of the last game, and ghosts
of friends if no one is live, so the experience degrades gracefully
instead of dropping you into an empty lobby.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Users valued "live" features that allow them to join friends' games
immediately without waiting. Most prefer playing with friends, but
some would play live with strangers if no one was around, similar to
Roblox.
</Quote>

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" title="Games Presence, so an empty lobby is the exception" hideFromNav>
Live multiplayer only works if you can find a friend playing. I
designed Games Presence UI to surface the bitmoji and name of
friends currently in a multiplayer game, in friends feed and in
chat itself. Tapping a presence chip drops you straight into the
session as a spectator if you haven't joined yet. The chip was the
smallest piece of UI that made the framework feel alive.
</Section>

associated with games in order to know their friends are interested

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 2" title="Pulling turn-based all the way off the Snapping framework" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The early-bets turn-based fixes made the existing format less broken.
With Connected Lenses for Multiplayer now shipped, the full rewrite
was finally possible. I moved turn-based onto the live multiplayer
format: turns auto-send when you play them, the opponent can watch a
replay, and the game flips into a live session the moment both
players are present.

The chat envelope is no longer a wrapper around a Snap: it's a
real-time surface that shows whose turn it is, updates as the game
state changes, and has a Remind CTA to nudge the other player
without leaving chat. A turn becomes a message, not a Snap.
</Section>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Some users did not think games could be tied to Snapping, as it's
more of a watching experience than playing with friends.
</Quote>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 3" title="A stationary entry on Discover" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
Discover gave us the traffic for Job 2, but we had to pick a
treatment that didn't break the feed. I worked through three options
with the team: a pinned pill (confusing UX), a pinned tile (too
disruptive to the page), and a pinned carousel component. We aligned
on the carousel: it lives inside the existing visual rhythm of the
page, gives solo players a predictable place to land, and quietly
becomes a natural home for playable game ads down the line.

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 4" title="A daily mechanism for solo players" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
With retentive game lenses already at around 30% D1, a daily
mechanism was the obvious next lever for Job 2. The design proposal:
a Daily Challenges row pinned to the front of the Games carousel, a
per-game streak, an auto-Snap to Preview the day's result, and
notifications that fire only on social signal (a friend playing the
same puzzle), not on the streak itself.

The team pushed back on a few of my early proposals, including a
per-game permission widget, and we landed on a single opt-in modal
the first time you enter a game. Those calls came out of design
review and made the final flow noticeably cleaner.
</Section>

<Section stage="Design" eyebrow="Project 5" title="Defending the camera for creation" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Design" hideFromNav>
The Lens carousel kept attracting long-play content: longer-form
games, playable ads, choose-your-own-adventure stories. I drove a
content strategy realignment with the cross-functional teams: the
camera should be for creation, and content earns its place in the
carousel only if it drives a Snap create, send, post, or save.
Long-play content lives on Discover and Spotlight instead.

As a follow-on, I scoped a Games Spotlight tab with
continue-where-you-left-off and a Browse Games entry point from any
fullscreen game unlock, so a player who arrives via a friend's score
can pivot into the broader catalog.
</Section>
