<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Games were just Lenses in that carousel" hideFromNav>
Games were a subset of lenses, mixed into the same carousel as face
filters and world effects. There was no dedicated category for them,
no search that reliably surfaced them, and most people did not know
the names of specific games anyway. You found one by stumbling across
it while browsing. The experiences were hyper-casual: short,
single-screen, pick-up-and-play, and almost entirely solo.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Background" title="Social games existed, but were limited" hideFromNav>
There was technically a way to play with friends: open a game lens,
play your turn, Snap the result, send it to someone. Your friend
opened the Snap, saw your static score, then found and opened the
same lens to take their turn.

The whole flow ran on Snapchat's record-and-send framework with no
dedicated invitation UI and no in-product explanation of the loop.
Users who stumbled on a game in the lens carousel saw something that
looked like a game, but had no way to know multiplayer was possible,
let alone how to start it.
</Section>

<Section stage="Research" eyebrow="Research" title="Pressure-testing the hypotheses with user research" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Research" title="Pressure-testing the hypotheses with user research" hideFromNav>
I partnered with our UXR team on a foundational gaming study: 1:1
interviews with 11 Snapchatters ages 13-24, half regular Lens-game
players, half not, all of them also playing on at least one
competitor platform (iMessage, Messenger, Roblox, Instagram, TikTok).
The study set out to validate or kill the bets I was making on the
direction.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={3}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 1" title="The mental-model split was real" tone="outline">
    "Users familiar with Lens games did not strongly associate filter
    games with Snapchat games, because it wasn't clear they could play
    games with friends in chat like on other platforms."
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 2" title="Users expected games in chat" tone="outline">
    "Users had difficulty finding games due to buried entry points...
    Users expected to start games within the chat." Most participants
    would initiate a game by asking a friend in chat first.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Finding 3" title="People wanted to play with friends" tone="outline">
    The most-appealing formats were social ones: live multiplayer,
    goal-oriented multiplayer, turn-based, party games. Solo or
    mindless formats ranked lowest.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Research" hideFromNav>
The research gave the work qualitative grounding to sit alongside the
engagement data. Specific findings show up next to the decisions they
validated, later in the case study.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Research" title="Two jobs to be done" hideFromNav>
I framed the work for the team around two jobs games could do for
Snap, and used that framing in exec reviews to scope the year.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 1" title="Connections and Conversations" tone="outline">
    Get friends to play together inside Snapchat. Games as a reason to
    open a chat, then to keep that chat going.
  </Callout>
  <Callout eyebrow="Job 2" title="Growth and Resurrection" tone="outline">
    Get more Snapchatters to open the app each day, especially users
    with a weaker social graph who aren't pulled in by chat alone.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section stage="Initial improvements" eyebrow="Project 2" title="Fullscreen game UX" />

<Section size="sub" stage="Initial improvements" hideFromNav>
  <Callout eyebrow="Problem" tone="outline">
    Games were built on the Lens framework, which meant they ran on top of the camera UI. For simple tap-to-play games that wasn't a problem, but for anything with moderate gameplay mechanics, the camera controls got in the way. The UI said "AR filter" when the experience was trying to say "game."
  </Callout>
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Initial improvements" hideFromNav>
Because games were user-generated content, any given lens could use any mechanics, gestures, or on-screen content in any position. The solution couldn't be bespoke: it had to work universally, without per-game configuration.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Initial improvements" hideFromNav>
I redesigned the game lens experience to open fullscreen on start
and unified the capture control: a single tap starts the game
silently, a long-press starts the game and starts hands-free
recording, and the round auto-progresses to Preview when it ends
so the clip is ready to send without leaving the game flow.
</Section>

<Section stage="Initial improvements" eyebrow="Project 3" title="Turn-based games, made less broken" />

<Section size="sub" stage="Initial improvements" hideFromNav>
  <Callout eyebrow="Problem" tone="outline">
    The turn-based flow was built on Snapchat's record-and-send framework with no dedicated UI, no invitation mechanic, and no in-product explanation of how it worked. Most users who stumbled on a game had no idea how to start a game.
  </Callout>

<TwoUp ratio="1:1" align="center">
  <Fragment slot="left">
**Auto-capture at the end of your turn.** The biggest reason turn-based
games never got off the ground was that nothing told you to send your
turn to a friend. I added auto-capture: when your turn ends, we
capture it automatically and prompt you to send it, making the send
step explicit instead of invisible.

<Section size="sub" stage="Initial improvements" hideFromNav>
**Snap envelope updates.** In a busy chat, turn-based game snaps blended

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="The exec ask: how do we go big with games?" banner />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
The outstanding engagement signals we saw from just three projects
were enough. Evan, the CEO, walked over and asked: **"how can we go
big with games?"** What follows is the answer I arrived at, and the
dedicated Games squad that came out of it.
</Section>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="First pitch: an arcade economy" />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
The first answer I tried to give was that Snap needed its own
arcade economy: one loop that monetized the games library and
pulled people deeper into Snap itself. Score points by playing
games. Spend those points on items in a global Snap storefront.
Run out, and you top up with cash or keep playing to earn more.
Rinse, repeat.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
The exec read came back fast: the path to monetization was murky,
and the pitch wasn't grounded in any industry data. Evan's gut
was that the current hyper-casual library wasn't the right base
to monetize this way long term, that pay-to-play on top of
pickup-and-drop games isn't where the money in mobile gaming is.

He was right. I'd designed an answer before I understood the
market. So I stopped pitching and went to study how the
established mobile gaming companies actually make money.
</Section>

<Section stage="Strategy" eyebrow="Strategy" title="The category bet" />

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" hideFromNav>
The study surfaced the insight that anchored the deck: Snap was
sitting in hyper-casual, but the industry's actual sweet spot is
hybrid-casual, the pick-up-and-play mechanics of hyper-casual
layered with the meta systems of mid-core (progression, persistence,
narrative). It was the part of the market growing 30%+ year-on-year,
and Snap's social graph was the unfair advantage that would make it
ours to win.
</Section>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="The four pillars" hideFromNav>
From the principles, four pillars to focus the next year of work
against. The deck assigned a clear design direction to each.
</Section>

<Callouts cols={2}>
  <Callout number="1" title="Monetize existing games">
    Grow Lens+ subscriptions, layer in video and rewarded ads on
    hyper-casual games using formats we already had, instead of
    building net-new ad surfaces.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="2" title="Enable hybrid-casual mechanics">
    Multiplayer as the foundation. Add progression, persistence, and
    a cross-game identity so a session is worth coming back to.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="3" title="Incentivize the developer ecosystem">
    Expand the Partner Developer Program with upfront funding for
    first-time devs and a discovery boost gated on game retention,
    playtime, and social mechanics.
  </Callout>
  <Callout number="4" title="Diversify monetization">
    Beyond ads: in-app purchases for progression boosters, plus
    Bitmoji cosmetics that persist across every game on Snapchat.
  </Callout>
</Callouts>

<Section size="sub" stage="Strategy" title="Pillar 3 in the deck: a flywheel for developers" hideFromNav>

<Quote attribution="Gaming Foundational Research, Nov 2025">
Users valued "live" features that allow them to join friends' games immediately without waiting. Most prefer playing with friends, but some would play live with strangers if no one was around, similar to Roblox.

<Section size="sub" stage="Post-strategy work" title="Three modes, one framework" hideFromNav>

Sessions support spectators, replays, and ghosts of friends so the experience degrades gracefully instead of dropping you into an empty lobby.
</Section>
